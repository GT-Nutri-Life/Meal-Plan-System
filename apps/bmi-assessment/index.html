Understand this result

? How BMI and the healthy range are calculated

BMI compares weight to height. It is a screening measure that flags whether weight is likely to be affecting health. It does not diagnose anything on its own.

Body mass index

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

Healthy weight range

Convert height to metres, multiply it by itself, then multiply the result by the lowest and highest healthy BMI values.

⚡ How BMR and daily energy are calculated

Basal metabolic rate is the energy the body uses at complete rest to keep the heart, brain, kidneys and other organs running. It is the largest single part of daily energy use, typically 60 to 70 per cent of the total.

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation predicts it from weight, height, age and sex.

Female: BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Male:    BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) + 5

Enter age and sex to see this worked through with the current measurements.

Daily total

Multiplying BMR by an activity factor gives total daily energy expenditure, the amount needed to hold weight steady at that level of activity.

Age lowers the figure by 5 kcal per year, which is why the same height and weight predicts a lower requirement later in life. Weight has the largest effect, at 10 kcal per kilogram.

kg The weight-based method, and which weight to use

Rather than predicting resting expenditure and multiplying it up, this method applies a single kcal-per-kilogram coefficient chosen for the clinical situation. It is quicker, needs no age or sex, and is what most units fall back on when indirect calorimetry is not available.

The trade-off is precision. Age and sex are folded into the coefficient rather than calculated, which is why every row is a range rather than a number.

Which weight the coefficient is applied to

This choice moves the answer more than the coefficient does, so it is worth being deliberate about.

Actual — the usual choice below a BMI of 30.

Ideal (Devine) — 45.5 kg female or 50 kg male, + 2.3 kg per inch above 60 in.

Adjusted — IBW + factor × (actual − IBW), with the factor between 0.25 and 0.4. Used at a BMI of 30 and above so that excess fat mass is counted in part rather than in full.

Oedema, ascites, significant fluid shifts or amputation all make the scale weight misleading. Estimate a dry or adjusted weight first, then apply the coefficient to that.

Reading the two methods together

A predictive figure that falls inside the weight-based range is mutual confirmation. A wide gap is a prompt, not an error: usually the activity factor is too generous, or a standard coefficient has been applied to actual weight at a high BMI. Whichever figure is chosen, it is a starting point to be titrated against measured weight change over the following days or weeks.

    ⇄ Which cut-off points to apply

    Cardiometabolic risk rises at a lower BMI in many Asian populations, so the WHO publishes a second set of public-health action points alongside the international classification. This tool applies the Asia-Pacific points by default; switch to the international set for populations where those are the reference.

    ! Where BMI and energy estimates mislead

    BMI cannot distinguish fat from lean mass. Muscular builds read high; sarcopenic older adults can read normal while carrying excess fat.

    It ignores fat distribution. Central adiposity carries greater risk, which is why waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio are measured alongside it.

    It is validated for adults aged 20 and over. Children and adolescents require age- and sex-specific percentile charts.

    It does not apply in pregnancy, and is unreliable with oedema, ascites, amputation or significant fluid shifts.

    It is population-derived. Body composition at a given BMI varies with ethnicity and age.

    BMR equations predict, they do not measure. Mifflin-St Jeor lands within 10 per cent of measured resting expenditure for most healthy adults, but it uses total weight rather than lean mass, so it tends to overestimate at very high body weight and underestimate in muscular builds.

    Activity factors are broad brackets. Two people who describe the same week can differ by several hundred kilocalories a day. Treat the figure as a starting point to be adjusted against real-world change.

    ✚ When to seek clinical review

    Arrange an appointment for any of these.

    • • Unintentional weight change
    • • Breathlessness, chest pain or leg swelling
    • • Persistent thirst, frequent urination or fatigue
    • • Loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas or daytime sleepiness
    • • Joint pain limiting daily activity
    • • Distress about weight, eating or body image

    Useful questions to bring

    • • Is BMI a fair measure for my build and age?
    • • Should blood pressure, glucose and lipids be checked?
    • • What weight should I aim for, and over what timeframe?
    • • What daily intake is appropriate for me, given my health and activity?
    § Formulae, conversions and sources

    Conversion factors used: 1 in = 2.54 cm exactly · 1 m = 100 cm · 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg exactly · 1 kg = 1000 g · 1 st = 14 lb · 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. Height and weight are held internally in cm and kg, so switching units never accumulates rounding error.

    BMI = kg ÷ m². Classification: WHO Technical Report 894 and the WHO expert consultation on Asian populations, 2004.

    BMR, Mifflin-St Jeor = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) − 161 for females, or + 5 for males. Mifflin et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 1990. Derived in healthy adults aged 19 to 78.

    Daily total = BMR × activity factor. Sedentary 1.2 · lightly active 1.375 · moderately active 1.55 · very active 1.725.

    Weight-based method = kcal/kg coefficient × the chosen body weight. Weight reduction 20–25 · maintenance 25–30 · repletion 30–35 (general dietetic practice). Critical illness 25–30 · hypocaloric at BMI 30–50, 11–14 on actual weight · hypocaloric above BMI 50, 22–25 on ideal weight (ASPEN/SCCM critical care nutrition guidelines, 2016). Refeeding risk 10–15, high risk 5–10, as a starting rate (NICE CG32). Coefficients differ between ASPEN, ESPEN and local policy — check against the guideline in use.

    Adjusted body weight for dosing energy = IBW + factor × (actual − IBW), factor selectable between 0.25 and 0.4.

    BSA Mosteller = √(height cm × weight kg ÷ 3600). Du Bois = 0.007184 × height0.725 × weight0.425.

    Ideal body weight, Devine = 45.5 kg (female) or 50 kg (male) + 2.3 kg per inch above 60 in. Unreliable below 152 cm.

    Adjusted body weight = IBW + 0.4 × (actual − IBW), shown when actual weight exceeds 120% of IBW.

    Waist thresholds: WHO, increased risk from 94 cm (male) and 80 cm (female); substantially increased from 102 cm and 88 cm. Asia-Pacific: 90 cm and 80 cm.

    Waist-to-height ratio target below 0.50, per NICE CG189 guidance on central adiposity.
